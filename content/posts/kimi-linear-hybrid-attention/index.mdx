# Kimi Linear: when hybrid linear attention actually wins

Linear attention used to be the thing you tried when softmax got expensive, and then quietly regretted. The recall curves were worse, the long-context benchmarks were worse, and the only thing going for it was the scaling story. Every year a new paper promised parity, and every year somebody ran a needle-in-a-haystack and the field went back to softmax.

Then I read [Kimi Linear](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26692) (2025) and had to update. The folklore ("linear attention loses on recall") is a statement about 2021-era linear attention. [Gated DeltaNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06464) (2024) and then KDA quietly fixed it, and the cache math on the other side is really clean.

softmax attention (2017) → linear attention (2020) → [RetNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08621) (2023) → [GLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06635) (2023) → [DeltaNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06484) (2024) → Gated DeltaNet (2024) → Kimi Linear / KDA (Oct 2025) → 2026+ wave.

## The cache math nobody talks about

Forget quality for a second. At 1M context, decoding is memory-bound on a KV cache that's become bigger than the model's weights. For MHA on an illustrative 48-layer, 32-head, 128-dim-per-head stack at bf16 (numbers picked to match the plot defaults, not a Kimi Linear spec), you're reading ~800 GB per generated token just to look at history. That doesn't fit on an H100, let alone run fast on one. GQA buys you a group-size factor. In [DeepSeek-V2-style MLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434) (2024) the cached state is a low-rank latent plus a tiny decoupled RoPE channel, and that's already a 10–15× win over MHA. Good, but still linear in context. (Kimi Linear's MLA layers actually apply NoPE, not RoPE, so the decoupled-RoPE channel drops out for them — the post's compute keeps a small RoPE channel in the MLA term to match the more common DeepSeek-V2 pattern; it's a second-order effect on the plot shape.)

The Kimi Linear hybrid uses MLA on only 1 in 4 layers (the paper's 3:1 KDA-to-MLA ratio, confirmed in the abstract-adjacent contributions and in the ablation table). The other three layers carry a KDA recurrent state that's *constant in context*. Stack the two kinds of layers and the cache-vs-context curve goes from a single steep line to a shallow line plus a baseline offset.

At the default numbers (small-dense Kimi-style, 1M context, 3:1 ratio) the hybrid is about 4× smaller than pure MLA and tiny compared to MHA. The paper's headline number is "up to 75% KV-cache reduction", which is what the summary under the plot ticks over to. That reduction is the 4× in disguise. Slide `ratio` to 0 (pure MLA) to see where you'd be without it; slide it to 8 and you're basically pure KDA with one global-attention layer still around to do exact-recall work.

<Callout tone="info" title="Why the hybrid curve has a baseline">
  A KDA layer stores an O(h · d · d) recurrent state per layer regardless of
  context. That's the flat part of the curve. An MLA layer stores O(d_c · T)
  per layer, which scales with context T. In the 3:1 hybrid, three quarters
  of the stack is flat and one quarter is sloped. The slope is the slope
  that matters at long context.
</Callout>

## The throughput payoff

Decoding is memory-bound on HBM. Each step the GPU streams the KV cache plus the weights across the bus, and the arithmetic is essentially free. So the decode rate at context T is roughly

$$
\text{tokens/sec} \approx \frac{B_{\text{HBM}}}{\text{KV}(T) + W}
$$

where W is the weight footprint (shared across batch). At 1M context on a 48B-total / 3B-active Kimi-style stack, KV dominates the denominator for MHA and is comparable to W for MLA. The hybrid collapses KV to a constant offset, so the denominator barely grows with context at all.

The paper reports "up to 6× decoding throughput for a 1M context" against full attention (their Figure 1b actually shows 6.3× vs MLA at 1M). My toy roofline at batch 1 lands closer to 13× vs raw MHA because it ignores arithmetic overhead and uses MHA as the baseline instead of the paper's stronger MLA baseline. The shape is what to trust, not the absolute multiplier: MHA collapses toward zero as context grows, the hybrid barely moves. Everything downstream (serving cost, per-token latency, concurrent-session density) inherits the same factor.

## What KDA actually is

Linear attention replaces softmax with a kernel feature map φ so that attention is $\mathrm{softmax}(QK^T)V \to \phi(Q)(\phi(K)^T V)$. The associativity lets you keep a running state $S_t = S_{t-1} + \phi(k_t) v_t^T$ and read it with $o_t = \phi(q_t)^T S_t$. Constant state per layer, O(T) total compute, pretty on paper. In practice S fills up and reads smear across everything you've seen.

The fixes came in two waves:

1. **A forget gate.** GLA and RetNet multiplied S by a decay each step. Helps, but a scalar gate has to pick one timescale for the whole state.
2. **A delta rule.** DeltaNet, then Gated DeltaNet, added an associative-memory update: instead of writing `φ(k)v^T`, write the *error* between what the state currently returns for k and the new value v. The Widrow-Hoff update from classical linear associative memory. Clears conflicts.

Kernelised Delta Attention (KDA) takes Gated DeltaNet's scalar gate and makes it *per-channel*. Every dimension of the recurrent state gets its own forget rate. Some channels hold for thousands of steps; others flush every few. The paper uses a specialised Diagonal-Plus-Low-Rank transition matrix so it stays cheap.

The update, roughly:

$$
S_t = \alpha_t \odot \left( S_{t-1} - \beta_t \left( S_{t-1} k_t - v_t \right) k_t^\top \right)
$$

where $\alpha_t$ is the vector per-channel gate (KDA) or a scalar (Gated DeltaNet), and $\beta_t$ is the delta-rule step size. Same structure, finer gating.

That's the whole conceptual move. Let the model decide, per channel, whether this is a "hold for a long time" dimension or a "flush every few steps" one. Scalar gates can't do that (they have to pick a compromise). Per-channel gates let the state act more like cache-with-priorities and less like a leaky bucket.

<Callout tone="insight" title="Capacity wasn't the bottleneck; allocation was">
  The 2021-era complaint was that a linear-attention state isn't big enough to
  hold long-range content. It's actually plenty big. The state vector has
  thousands of channels. The real problem was that every channel decayed at
  the same rate. Per-channel gating is the difference between "state with a
  ton of capacity I can't target" and "state with useful allocation".
</Callout>

## The ratio

KDA-only is tempting (constant cache, great throughput) but pure-linear models still lose on tasks where exact retrieval matters. Pure MLA is the opposite. The interesting question is how few global-attention layers you actually need to get the recall back. The paper's answer is 1 in 4 (their 3:1 ratio).

The recall curve here is a surrogate (my hand-tuned sigmoid, not a measurement). Treat the shape as the claim, not the absolute values. What's load-bearing: throughput rises monotonically in ratio, recall eventually falls off, and the knee where you've captured most of the throughput gain before recall buckles sits near 3–4. Crank `recallPenalty` up and the knee slides left; crank it down and it slides right. The paper's 3:1 is the empirical answer to this same trade-off.

<Callout tone="aside" title="Where the 3:1 probably came from">
  I don't think Moonshot swept a 2D grid of ratios. They probably found
  that 1:1 was wasteful (MLA is expensive and you don't need it every
  layer), 7:1 dropped recall, and 3:1 was a round number that worked.
  Ratios this clean usually come from "try the obvious ones, pick the
  best". I mean this kindly.
</Callout>

## What actually changed

If you'd asked me a year ago whether linear attention was going to matter for production LLMs, I would have said "not this decade". That call was wrong. Three things flipped it:

- **Per-channel gating.** A boring-sounding change with a large effect. State capacity was never the bottleneck; allocation was.
- **The delta rule.** Treating the recurrent state as an associative memory with error-correcting updates, rather than as a running average.
- **The hybrid.** Nobody actually needs to be purist. Keeping ~25% of layers as global attention covers exact-recall queries without giving up the cache budget.

Softmax attention isn't going anywhere (the hybrid still uses it, just sparingly). What's gone is the assumption that you need it on every layer. Most tokens most of the time don't want a full quadratic look-back. They want a state that can hold the few things that mattered, plus an occasional global-attention layer to reach across the whole window when it counts.

The cache math on the other side is really clean. That's the part I keep coming back to.
