Almost every quantization tutorial I've read ends the same way: pick a bit
width, run GPTQ or AWQ end-to-end, compare perplexity. The implied model is
that the network is homogeneous (twiddle one global knob, move along a
one-dimensional accuracy/size frontier). That's a perfectly fine first
approximation and a really bad second one.

Layers differ. Attention projections, MLP up-projections, the LM head, the
first and last blocks all have different weight distributions, different
activation statistics, and wildly different sensitivity to rounding. Quantize
them all at the same width and you're over-spending bits on the easy layers
and starving the hard ones. The real question is: **given a bit budget, where
do the bits go?**

(A note on the plots before we start: everything in this post is a toy model
with the right shape, not a measurement on a real checkpoint. The shapes are
the point.)

<Callout tone="insight" title="Quantization as allocation">
  Quantization isn't a scalar. It's an allocation problem across layers. The
  uniform answer is the baseline; mixed precision is the optimisation on top,
  and below 4 bits average it stops being optional.
</Callout>

## Uniform quantization is lazy

Uniform int4 is the default because it's easy. One scale, one zero-point per
tensor (or per channel), one kernel, one story in the release notes. It also
happens to work surprisingly well down to about 4 bits on most modern LLMs:
[GPTQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323) (2022) and
[AWQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978) (2023) both beat naive round-to-nearest
by a wide margin while keeping the whole model at one width.

But the reason it works at 4 bits is that 4 bits is generous enough that the
worst layers still survive. Push the average below 4 and a few layers explode,
the rest barely change, and perplexity takes an elbow upward. Those few layers
carry most of the model's representational mass, and you've been rounding them
as if they were the middle of a residual MLP.

Empirically, when you plot real sensitivity (the Hessian diagonal estimate
GPTQ uses internally, or the activation outlier norms that
[SmoothQuant](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438) (2022) leans on, or the
outlier-feature analysis from [LLM.int8()](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07339)
(2022)), it looks much like this, give or take a few idiosyncratic spikes.
The tail layers matter more than the count of them would suggest: the top
10–20% of layers by sensitivity routinely account for more than half the total
quant error at a fixed uniform width.

<Callout tone="warn" title="Sensitivity vs. error contribution">
  A layer with sensitivity s_i contributes s_i · 2^(-2b) error at bit width
  b. That's exponential in b. Losing a bit from a hard layer costs 4× the
  error it would have cost on an easy layer. Uniform quantization is a
  failure to charge for this.
</Callout>

## The bit-budget optimisation

Formalise it. For a network of $N$ layers with sensitivities $\{s_i\}$, the
toy total quant error is

$$
E(\mathbf{b}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} s_i \cdot 2^{-2 b_i},
$$

where $b_i$ is the bit width assigned to layer $i$. The constraint is an
average budget, $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i b_i = B$. Minimise $E$ subject to that.

The Lagrangian has a clean solution: at the optimum, the marginal error of
spending one more bit is the same on every layer. Call that marginal cost
$\nu$ (a "water level") and the optimum is

$$
b_i^\star = \tfrac{1}{2} \log_2\!\left(\frac{s_i}{\nu}\right) + \text{const},
$$

clamped to whatever $[b_{\min}, b_{\max}]$ bounds you have for hardware reasons.
Binary-search $\nu$ until the constraint holds. The picture is pouring water
over a terrain whose elevation is $-\log_2 s_i$ (hence "water filling"). The
math here is pretty. It's also the solver I actually reach for in
`parameter-golf`, where the per-deployment bit allocation matters more than
any fancier loss surrogate.

The summary under the plot reports the achieved average (it matches the target

At $B = 4$ the reduction is modest. At $B = 3$ it's large. At $B = 2.5$ it's

<Callout tone="info" title="Same move as rate-distortion allocation">
  Water-filling is textbook in source coding: allocate rate to subbands
  proportional to their variance. Here the "subbands" are layers and the
  "variances" are sensitivities. The logic survives unchanged: exponential
  cost in $b$, additive budget in $b$, so logs drop out and the optimum is a
  simple shift.
</Callout>

## The perplexity/bits frontier

Uniform-at-average-$B$ is one point. Mixed precision at the same average is
another. The gap between them is the whole argument: at high $B$ they
converge, at low $B$ they diverge hard.

A toy relationship that tracks real curves in the interesting regime is
$\mathrm{ppl}(b) = \mathrm{ppl}_{\text{fp16}} \cdot (1 + \alpha \cdot
2^{-\beta b})$. Apply it per-layer for the mixed allocation, apply it
uniformly for the baseline, and read off the gap.

Some things to try:

1. Slide the highlighted budget 4 bits → 3 bits. The vertical gap between
   uniform and mixed widens faster than the budget is shrinking, because the
   uniform curve is already into its elbow.
2. Crank `spread` up. More heterogeneity means more room for mixed precision
   to help. If every layer were identical, the two curves would coincide
   exactly (there's nothing to allocate differently between clones).
3. Pull `β` down to ~0.6. Now bits don't buy much for any layer; both curves go
   shallow and the gap nearly closes. "How much does a bit cost?" is really
   a statement about $β$.

## Hardware constraints aren't neutral

So far this has been a paper exercise where bits are interchangeable. On real
GPUs they aren't. INT4 tensor cores are genuinely faster than FP16 ones
(that's the whole point of quantizing), but the moment you mix precisions
inside a forward pass you pay for conversions: dequant kernels at FP16
boundaries, extra memory traffic moving scales around, and (on older
hardware) compute that won't fuse cleanly into the matmul epilogue.

The dumb model: every layer costs $T_{\text{int4}}$ if it's in INT4 and
$T_{\text{fp16}}$ if it's in FP16, and every transition between them costs
$C$. If you interleave FP16 layers randomly among INT4 ones at fraction $f$,
the expected number of transitions is $\sim 2f(1-f)N$, maximised at $f=0.5$
(the worst case).

Two takeaways the plot makes concrete:

- At small $f$, the FP16 layers are cheap enough that the conversion tax is
  dwarfed by the accuracy they buy back. This is the regime the bit-budget
  optimiser almost always wants to live in.
- Clustering your FP16 layers (say, keeping them all at the ends of the
  stack) cuts the number of precision transitions to $O(1)$ instead of
  $O(fN)$. That's probably a better real-world strategy than "exactly the
  top-k by sensitivity, scattered through the stack", even if the latter
  is what pure water-filling suggests. In `parameter-golf` I treat the
  clustering constraint as a first-class term in the objective rather than
  a post-hoc patch, for exactly this reason.

<Callout tone="aside" title="The clamps carry the decision">
  In real deployments `maxBits` is often pinned at 8 (16 is a waste if the
  kernel supports 8) and `minBits` at 3 (INT2 kernels are either hypothetical
  or much slower). Those two numbers, not the clever middle of the allocator,
  are what turn a beautiful Pareto curve into a deployable config.
</Callout>
