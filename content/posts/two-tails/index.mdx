# Two tails: saturation and quantization

Two of the failure modes I see most often in model-serving postmortems have
nothing in common on the surface. One is a queuing problem: the box is fine at
the mean, but p99 latency explodes as we push utilisation towards 1. The other
is a numerics problem: the model is fine at full precision, but a quantized
copy starts disagreeing with it on a small subset of inputs and the top-1
accuracy drifts. Different teams, different dashboards, different oncalls.

And yet both are **tail phenomena** — the mean is benign, the interesting mass
is at the edge of the distribution — and the intuition from one transfers
cleanly to the other. This post walks through both, with the same two knobs on
every plot: crank them, watch the tail move.

<Callout tone="insight" title="The thing to take away">
  In a production system, the mean is a soothing lie. Capacity planning lives
  in the tail of the response-time distribution; accuracy of a quantized model
  lives in the tail of the per-weight perturbation distribution. If your
  monitoring only shows averages, both failure modes will surprise you.
</Callout>

## Tail one: queue saturation

A single request-serving replica, in the limit where requests are
memoryless and service time is exponential, is textbook M/M/1. Arrivals at
rate $\lambda$, service at rate $\mu$, one server, infinite queue.

<Figure
  src="/posts/two-tails/mm1-queue.svg"
  alt="A single-server queue with arrivals at rate lambda, a waiting line, a server at rate mu, and departures."
  caption="An M/M/1 replica: one server, one queue, utilisation ρ = λ/μ."
/>

The survival function of the response time $T$ — the probability that a given
request takes longer than $t$ — has a very tidy closed form:

$$
P(T > t) = e^{-(\mu - \lambda)\, t},
$$

which is _only_ well-defined when $\lambda < \mu$. As $\rho = \lambda / \mu$
approaches 1, the exponent $\mu - \lambda$ collapses towards zero and the
survival curve flattens — a unit of time out in the tail carries far more
probability than the mean would suggest. The p99 moves faster than the
average.

Two experiments worth running in the plot above:

1. Pin service time at 6 ms and slide the arrival rate from 80 → 160 qps. The
   mean barely moves; the area past the SLA grows non-linearly.
2. Hold the arrival rate steady and cut service time by 1 ms. Watch the whole
   curve pull left. A tiny improvement in the body of the distribution buys a
   large improvement in the tail — which is the classic argument for sweating
   the small optimisations in hot code paths.

<Callout tone="aside" title="Why M/M/1 is a lie you can use">
  Real traffic is bursty, not Poisson. Real service time is rarely exponential.
  Real load balancers spread across N replicas, not 1. All true. But the shape
  of the result — that utilisation past ~0.8 is where pain compounds — survives
  every generalisation I have reached for. M/M/1 is the minimum-viable tool for
  building the intuition. Keep it; swap it for something richer only when a
  decision actually hinges on the richer model.
</Callout>

## Tail two: quantization flips

Now the other kind of tail. Take a trained weight $w$ (in grid units, so the
quantization grid lives at the integers) and add a small Gaussian perturbation
$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. Round-to-nearest quantization snaps
$w + \epsilon$ to the nearest integer level. The weight _flips_ — rounds to a
different level than it would have without the perturbation — exactly when
$|\epsilon| > d(w)$, where $d(w)$ is the distance from $w$ to the nearest
half-integer boundary.

The per-weight flip probability is therefore

$$
p_\text{flip}(w) = 2\left(1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{d(w)}{\sigma}\right)\right),
$$

which is smallest for weights that sit near the centre of a bin ($d(w)$ large)
and largest for weights that sit right on a boundary ($d(w) \to 0$, so
$p_\text{flip} \to 1$).

Things to notice:

- The curve has a tooth for every bin boundary. Drop bits from 4 to 2 and the
  teeth move wider apart (the grid coarsens) but each tooth has the same
  peak — because the geometry near a boundary is local.
- Raise $\sigma$ past roughly half a grid unit and every tooth touches 1.
  At that point the grid has stopped mattering: the noise dominates the
  quantization. This is the regime where aggressive bitwidth reduction quietly
  degrades accuracy on the hard examples while the average-case metric stays
  flat.

## Why the analogy is more than cute

Superficially these are two different animals: one lives in a load balancer,
the other in a kernel. But in each case the median behaviour is fine, the
metric you are optimising is also fine, and the damage shows up in the shape of
the distribution, at the edge, on a subset of the inputs that happen to land in
the bad region. If you treat them the same way at design time — plot the tail,
pick a threshold, budget for it — you get fewer surprises at 3 a.m.

## The knobs that actually help

- For queue saturation: provision for **utilisation**, not for average QPS.
  Targeting $\rho = 0.7$ instead of $0.9$ costs more boxes but dramatically
  shrinks the area past your SLA.
- For quantization flips: don't pick bits by measuring average loss. Measure
  **flip rate** at the weights that sit near boundaries, and keep a higher
  precision (or a small FP16 fallback) for the layers where the flipping
  range swamps the safe region.

Both are the same move: refuse to let the mean set the budget.
