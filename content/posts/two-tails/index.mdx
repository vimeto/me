# Two tails: saturation and quantization

Two failure modes keep showing up in model-serving postmortems, and they look
unrelated until you plot them. One's a queuing problem (the box is fine at the
mean, but p99 latency explodes as utilisation climbs toward 1). The other's a
numerics problem (the model is fine at full precision, but a quantized copy
starts disagreeing with it on a small subset of inputs and top-1 drifts).
Different teams, different dashboards, different oncalls.

That they're the same shape surprised me. Both are **tail phenomena** (the
mean is benign, the interesting mass sits at the edge), and the intuition from
one transfers cleanly to the other. Same two knobs on every plot below: crank
them, watch the tail move.

<Callout tone="insight" title="Why averages miss both failures">
  The mean is a soothing lie. Capacity planning lives in the tail of the
  response-time distribution; accuracy of a quantized model lives in the tail
  of the per-weight perturbation distribution. If your monitoring only shows
  averages, both failure modes will surprise you.
</Callout>

## Tail one: queue saturation

A single request-serving replica, in the limit where requests are memoryless
and service time is exponential, is textbook M/M/1. Arrivals at rate $\lambda$,
service at rate $\mu$, one server, infinite queue.

<Figure
  src="/posts/two-tails/mm1-queue.svg"
  alt="A single-server queue with arrivals at rate lambda, a waiting line, a server at rate mu, and departures."
  caption="An M/M/1 replica: one server, one queue, utilisation ρ = λ/μ."
/>

The survival function of the response time $T$ (the probability a request
takes longer than $t$) has a tidy closed form:

$$
P(T > t) = e^{-(\mu - \lambda)\, t},
$$

which is _only_ well-defined when $\lambda < \mu$. As $\rho = \lambda / \mu$
approaches 1, the exponent $\mu - \lambda$ collapses toward zero and the
survival curve flattens. A unit of time out in the tail carries far more
probability than the mean would suggest. The p99 moves faster than the average.

Two experiments worth running in the plot above:

1. Pin service time at 6 ms and slide the arrival rate 80 → 160 qps. The mean
   barely moves; the area past the SLA grows non-linearly.
2. Hold the arrival rate steady and cut service time by 1 ms. Watch the whole
   curve pull left. A tiny improvement in the body buys a large improvement in
   the tail (the classic argument for sweating small optimisations in hot code
   paths).

<Callout tone="aside" title="Why M/M/1 is enough for intuition">
  Real traffic is bursty, not Poisson. Real service time is rarely exponential.
  Real load balancers spread across N replicas, not 1. All true. But the shape
  of the result (utilisation past ~0.8 is where pain compounds) survives every
  generalisation I've reached for. M/M/1 is the minimum-viable tool for the
  intuition. Keep it; swap for something richer only when a decision actually
  hinges on the richer model.
</Callout>

## Tail two: quantization flips

Now the other kind of tail. Take a trained weight $w$ in some fixed range
$[-R, R]$ and add a small Gaussian perturbation
$\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. A $b$-bit symmetric quantizer places
$L = 2^b$ grid levels across that range, so neighbouring levels are separated
by a step of $\Delta = 2R/(L-1)$. Round-to-nearest snaps $w + \epsilon$ to the
nearest level. The weight _flips_ (rounds to a different level than it would
have without the perturbation) exactly when $|\epsilon| > d(w)$, where $d(w)$
is the distance from $w$ to the nearest inter-level boundary.

The per-weight flip probability is therefore

$$
p_\text{flip}(w) = 2\left(1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{d(w)}{\sigma}\right)\right),
$$

smallest for weights that sit near the centre of a bin ($d(w)$ large) and
largest for weights that sit right on a boundary ($d(w) \to 0$, so
$p_\text{flip} \to 1$).

Things to notice:

- The curve has a tooth for every inter-level boundary. Drop bits 4 → 2 and
  the teeth move wider apart (the grid coarsens, $\Delta$ grows) but each
  tooth still peaks at 1, because at the boundary itself $d(w) = 0$ and the
  flip probability is always $2(1 - \Phi(0)) = 1$, regardless of spacing.
- Raise $\sigma$ past roughly $\Delta/2$ and every tooth touches 1. At that
  point the grid has stopped mattering: the noise dominates the quantization.
  This is the regime where aggressive bitwidth reduction quietly degrades
  accuracy on the hard examples while the average-case metric stays flat.

## Why the analogy is more than cute

On the surface these are two different animals: one lives in a load balancer,
the other in a kernel. But in both cases the median behaviour is fine, the
metric you're optimising is fine, and the damage shows up in the shape of the
distribution, at the edge, on a subset of inputs that happen to land in the bad
region. If you treat them the same way at design time (plot the tail, pick a
threshold, budget for it) you get fewer surprises at 3 a.m.

## The knobs that actually help

- For queue saturation: provision for **utilisation**, not for average QPS.
  Targeting $\rho = 0.7$ instead of $0.9$ costs more boxes but dramatically
  shrinks the area past your SLA.
- For quantization flips: don't pick bits by measuring average loss. Measure
  **flip rate** at the weights that sit near boundaries, and keep a higher
  precision (or a small FP16 fallback) for the layers where the flipping
  range swamps the safe region.

Both are the same move: refuse to let the mean set the budget.
