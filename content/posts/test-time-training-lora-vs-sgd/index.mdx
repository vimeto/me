# Test-time training: LoRA vs SGD

Most of what I do at inference is frozen weights and sampling. Test-time
training (TTT) is the other thing: the model gets to nudge its own parameters
on the prompt it's about to answer, either with a self-supervised objective
([Test-Time Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13231) (2020)) or with a fresh
inner-loop that treats each sequence like a tiny fine-tune (the
[TTT-layers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04620) (2024) paper makes this
explicit). You pay extra FLOPs per request; you hope the loss on _this_
example drops far enough to be worth it.

Two design choices dominate the cost: how many weights are you willing to
change, and how big are your steps. That's it, in four small plots.

<Callout tone="insight" title="LoRA at test time is a subspace bet">
  LoRA at test time is a rank budget on the update direction, not a cost
  rebate. You get most of the adaptation benefit if the thing that needs
  adapting already lives in a low-rank subspace, and very little of it if the
  gradient is isotropic. Pick the rank after you look at where the gradient
  mass actually lives.
</Callout>

## The premise: train a little, every time

A TTT-adapted model answers a prompt in two phases. First, a short inner loop
updates some subset of the weights with an auxiliary loss computed from the
prompt itself (predict held-out tokens, reconstruct a rotated image, minimise
next-token loss on the prompt, etc.). Second, the adapted model generates the
answer. The update is then discarded. Each prompt gets a freshly adapted copy,
and the base checkpoint never moves.

The original [Test-Time Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13231) paper
adapts the whole network with full SGD, one step per test image.
[TTT-layers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.04620) treats the hidden state _as_
a learnable model and does the inner loop inside the forward pass. The knob
common to every version is the same: how much of $W$ do you let move?

## Low-rank update geometry

[LoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685) (2021) constrains the update to a
rank-$r$ subspace:

$$
W_\text{adapted} = W + \Delta W, \quad \Delta W = B A,\quad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}, \; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d}.
$$

Whatever gradient signal you would have applied to the full $d\times d$ matrix
gets projected onto the column space of $B$ and the row space of $A$. So the
question "is LoRA enough?" is really "how much of $\nabla_W \mathcal{L}$ lives
in that subspace?"

For a random direction in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and a random rank-$r$ subspace, the
expected captured fraction is exactly $r/d$. For gradients that concentrate on
a few coordinates (anything that looks like "only a handful of neurons matter
for this example"), the number can be much higher.

Pin $d = 1024$ in the plot above and slide $\alpha$. At $\alpha = 0.2$ (nearly
isotropic) you need a rank in the hundreds to catch half the update; at
$\alpha = 1.5$ a rank of 16 gets you most of the way. This isn't a story about
whether LoRA works. It's a story about whether the _particular gradient you're
projecting_ has the structure LoRA likes.

<Callout tone="aside" title="Random subspace vs learned subspace">
  The $r/d$ result assumes the subspace is chosen before you look at the
  gradient. Standard LoRA fine-tuning _learns_ $A$ and $B$ from data, so the
  subspace aligns with directions the training loss actually cares about
  (empirically much better than random). TTT is different: the adaptation
  subspace has to be fixed before the test prompt arrives, or you pay an
  extra forward–backward pass to choose it. The isotropic lower bound is a
  reasonable worst case.
</Callout>

## Full SGD vs LoRA: two trajectories

Here's the toy version. Take the loss surface $f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 +
c\,y^2)$ with $c > 1$ (a stretched bowl, minimum at the origin). Full SGD from
some off-axis initial point zigzags a bit but gets there. LoRA rank-1 in 2D
means we project every gradient onto a fixed 1D direction $u = (\cos\theta,
\sin\theta)$. Pick $u$ poorly and the trajectory stops at whatever point on
the line $\text{span}(u)$ is closest to the initialisation. It can't make
progress off the line, ever.

Two quick experiments:

1. Set $\theta = 0$ and $c = 5$. The LoRA path shoots to $y = 1.2$ on the
   x-axis, hits $x = 0$, and _stops_. It's lost all ability to reduce $y$.
   Full SGD gets there in a zigzag (slowly, but it gets there).
2. Set $\theta = \arctan(1.2/1.6) \approx 0.64$ (aimed at the init vector) and
   watch the LoRA path go straight to the origin. The right subspace makes
   rank-1 indistinguishable from full.

That's the geometric claim: LoRA stalls iff the descent direction has a
persistent component orthogonal to the chosen subspace.

<Callout tone="warn" title="The test-time failure mode">
  At train time you get thousands of batches to correct a suboptimal LoRA
  subspace. At test time you get one prompt and a handful of inner steps. If
  the gradient at this particular example points somewhere the rank-r
  adapter can't reach, TTT-LoRA will confidently converge to a point that
  isn't a minimum, and you'll ship that adapted model's answer.
</Callout>

## The adaptation curve

Collapse the whole trajectory down to a single scalar (the loss on the
auxiliary objective) and watch it evolve as we take more inner steps. For a
convex proxy loss near a minimum, you expect

$$
\mathcal{L}(t) \approx \mathcal{L}_0 \cdot e^{-\eta\, t} + \varepsilon,
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the noise floor of whatever self-supervised signal
you're using. Too-small $\eta$: the curve decays slowly and you run out of
inference budget before it does anything useful. Too-big $\eta$: the inner
optimiser overshoots, the loss _grows_, and the adapted model is now strictly
worse than the base. There's a usable band in between.

The practical implication is sharper than "pick a learning rate". At train
time, a too-big $\eta$ just means noisy updates that a scheduler eventually
tames. At test time there's no scheduler and no next epoch. A single divergent
inner loop is a shipped bad answer. TTT serving wants $\eta$ slightly below
the largest stable value (not at the sweet spot, below the cliff).

## Compute budget: what it costs at serve time

TTT-LoRA's per-request cost is real: you do an extra forward pass on the
auxiliary loss, a backward pass through just the LoRA adapters, and an
optimiser step. If the base model forward takes $\ell_0$ ms and each unit of
adapter rank adds $c$ ms of extra forward+backward, we can model

$$
\ell(r) = \ell_0 + r\, c, \qquad q(r) = 1 - e^{-r/\tau},
$$

where $q$ is the adaptation quality gain and $\tau$ is the "characteristic
rank" at which most of the gain is already realised. The interesting object
is the Pareto curve of quality vs latency.

Two places this plot argues with intuition:

- The smallest rank that reaches 80% of the adaptation ceiling sits well
  short of $r_{\max}$ (around $1.6\,\tau$ for this saturation model), because
  latency grows linearly while $q(r)$ has diminishing returns. Serving TTT
  wants that rank, not the one at which quality maxes out. This is the knee
  my `parameter-golf` solver actually picks when it's sizing a deployment:
  the rank that maximises quality-per-ms, not the rank that maximises quality.
- If the per-rank cost is dominated by the backward pass (usually is), a
  small improvement in backward-pass throughput is worth more than a big
  improvement in forward-pass throughput. Inference accelerators designed
  around forward-only workloads have this exactly backwards for TTT.
