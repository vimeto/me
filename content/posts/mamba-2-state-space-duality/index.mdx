The thing that surprised me about [Mamba-2 / SSD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21060) (2024) is that the headline result isn't a new idea. It's an identity. SSMs and linear attention are the same matrix, just different indexing conventions. Once you see the matrix, the 2–8× speedup over [Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752) (2023)'s selective-scan kernel is a one-line consequence.

(To set expectations: this is me reading the paper and writing down the part that made it click, not research of mine. The only things I built are the toy plots, and they exist so you can poke the identity instead of taking my word for it.)

S4 (2021) → S4D (2022) → Mamba / selective SSM (Dec 2023) → Mamba-2 / SSD (May 2024) → 2024+ hybrids (Jamba 1.5, Kimi Linear 2025). The first three are "how do we make an SSM that competes with a transformer". Mamba-2 is where the authors stopped arguing for SSMs as an alternative and showed the debate was partly a naming problem. Write out the matrix an SSM builds and it's the matrix linear attention builds. The only question left is which kernel you ship.

## The causal mixing matrix

A causal transformer layer takes a sequence $x_1, \dots, x_L$ and produces outputs $y_t$ that are some mix of the earlier tokens. Write the mixing as a matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times L}$, lower-triangular, so $y = M x$. Softmax attention picks $M_{tj} = \text{softmax}(q_t^\top k_j / \sqrt{d})$ (row-normalised, dense below the diagonal). Linear attention drops the softmax: $M_{tj} = q_t^\top k_j$.

An SSM does something that looks totally different at first. It runs a recurrence $h_t = A h_{t-1} + B x_t$, $y_t = C h_t$. But unroll it and collect the contribution of $x_j$ to $y_t$:

$$
M_{tj} = C_t \, A^{t-j} \, B_j \quad \text{for } t \geq j.
$$

That's the same $L \times L$ matrix, just built from a recurrence instead of attention scores. And the off-diagonal blocks have a specific low-rank structure: for any two disjoint intervals $[a, b]$ and $[c, d]$ with $b < c$, the block $M_{[c:d], [a:b]}$ factors as $(\text{stuff for the right block}) \cdot A^{c-b} \cdot (\text{stuff for the left block})$. Everything flows through the state, which has dimension $N$. So the block has rank at most $N$, no matter how big it is.

Matrices with this property are called **semi-separable**. The first plot shows the structure: one row of $M$ in log magnitude, with the rank bound sitting flat underneath.

That's the whole structural claim. Semi-separable matrices have a fast parameterisation: $O(LN)$ numbers describe the whole $L \times L$ matrix, and you can multiply by one in $O(LN)$ time via the recurrence or in $O(L^2 / C \cdot N)$ time via a block decomposition where $C$ is the chunk size. The recurrence is the SSM view. The block decomposition is what SSD ships.

## The identity made interactive

If SSMs and linear attention really are the same matrix, you should be able to compute $y = Mx$ either way and get the same answer to machine precision. So let's do that. Build a toy SSM with state dim $N$, sequence length $L$, random input. Compute $y$ (a) by running the recurrence and (b) by materialising $M$ and doing the matmul. Plot the log absolute difference per position.

The curve sits on machine epsilon. Noise scales out (both paths are the same linear map, so it affects them identically). $N$ and $L$ are free. This is the identity, not a metaphor for the identity. Slide $N$ up and you're adding rank to $M$; slide $L$ up and you're making it bigger. The two computations still agree.

<Callout tone="insight" title="Softmax attention breaks semi-separability">
  Plain softmax attention is not semi-separable. The row-wise normalisation couples every entry in the row to every other entry, and the off-diagonal blocks don't factor through a finite-dimensional state. *Linear* attention drops softmax, gets the factorisation back, and sits inside the same family as SSMs. Mamba-2's paper makes this explicit: the SSD framework covers "structured masked attention", i.e. linear attention with a causal mask whose structure is semi-separable.
</Callout>

## Where the 2–8× comes from

Once you know $M$ is semi-separable, you get two algorithms for free. The recurrence is $O(LN)$ sequential work. That's great for autoregressive decode (one token at a time, $O(N)$ per step) but terrible for training because it can't fill a GPU (the length dimension is a dep chain). Softmax attention can fill a GPU but costs $O(L^2 d)$ FLOPs and, before [FlashAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135), $O(L^2)$ HBM traffic.

SSD's block decomposition sits between them. Chop the sequence into chunks of size $C$. Inside a chunk, you do a dense $C \times C$ matmul (the diagonal block of $M$ is small enough to materialise cheaply). Between chunks, you aggregate state once and pass it along (one $N$-dimensional vector per chunk boundary). The arithmetic becomes matmul-heavy and maps onto tensor cores. The paper reports two comparisons on this plot: 2–8× over Mamba-1's selective scan ("naive SSM scan" in the legend), and parity with FlashAttention at 2k tokens, 6× at 16k ("FlashAttention"). The SSD-vs-FlashAttention multiplier shown at the cursor is the second comparison.

A few things worth trying. Crank $L$ up and watch both attention curves sink while SSD barely moves (per-token KV traffic grows with $L$; SSD's state traffic doesn't). Slide $N$ up and SSD's parity point with FlashAttention slides right: more state means more bytes per token, and $N \approx 64$ is what lands parity near 2k. Pull bandwidth down and every curve sinks together except the naive scan, which is latency-bound and barely moves. Shrink $C$ and watch SSD's tail droop: fewer tokens per chunk means more state passes per token.

<Callout tone="info" title="Two algorithms for one matrix">
  There are really two algorithms, and you pick by sequence length. Short $L$ → use the quadratic-in-$L$ "attention-like" path (dense matmul on the whole triangle). Long $L$ → use the linear-in-$L$ "SSM-like" path (chunked state passing). Both compute the same matrix. Tri Dao's blog post calls this "having your cake and eating it too", which is almost too accurate.
</Callout>

## Selectivity survives

[Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752) (2023)'s contribution was making $A$ and $B$ depend on the input: $A_t$, $B_t$ instead of constants. That's what "selective" means in "selective SSM". It's also what made the parallel scan kernel hard, because a time-varying recurrence doesn't factor as a power of a matrix anymore. The worry before SSD came out was that the matmul reformulation would force everyone back to time-invariant dynamics.

It doesn't. The semi-separable structure survives when $A$ varies with $t$. The blocks still factor through an $N$-dimensional state; the factors just aren't powers of a single matrix anymore. SSD generalises to this case; the kernel gets a little more complex but the asymptotics are unchanged. Selectivity is what lets the state decide, per token, whether to remember or forget, and you can watch it happen in a toy recurrence.

At full selectivity the state pops up on spikes and collapses on zeros, while the non-selective one averages everything it ever saw. The Mamba-2 paper keeps this mechanism and restructures only the kernel; the model behaviour is continuous with Mamba-1.

<Callout tone="aside" title="Hybrid stacks in 2024+">
  Pure SSM stacks lost ground in 2024–2025 to hybrids that interleave a few softmax-attention layers among many SSM/linear-attention layers. Jamba 1.5 and Kimi Linear 2025 are the representative examples. The SSD identity matters here too: you can share a kernel between the "linear" layers even when their parameterisation comes from different families, because semi-separable is the common structure.
</Callout>

Writing this down changed the way I think about the attention-vs-SSM framing. There isn't one. There's a family of causal mixing matrices, softmax attention sits outside of it (the normalisation breaks the factorisation), and everything inside the family (linear attention, S4, Mamba, Mamba-2) is a choice of how to parameterise the same semi-separable structure and which kernel to ship. Cleaner than I thought, and more useful: once you have the matrix, the algorithm engineering is bounded.
