# Attention and SSMs are the same matrix

The thing that surprised me about [Mamba-2 / SSD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21060) (2024) is that it isn't a new idea. It's an identity. SSMs and linear attention are the same matrix, just different indexing conventions. Once you see the matrix, the 2–8× speedup over [Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752) (2023)'s selective-scan kernel is a one-line consequence.

S4 (2021) → S4D (2022) → Mamba / selective SSM (Dec 2023) → Mamba-2 / SSD (May 2024) → 2024+ hybrids (Jamba 1.5, Kimi Linear 2025). The first three are "how do we make an SSM that competes with a transformer". Mamba-2 is the one where the authors stopped arguing for SSMs as an alternative and showed that the debate was partly a naming problem. The matrix an SSM builds, when you write it out, is the same matrix linear attention builds. The only question is which kernel you ship.

## The causal mixing matrix

A causal transformer layer takes a sequence $x_1, \dots, x_L$ and produces outputs $y_t$ that are some mix of the earlier tokens. Write the mixing as a matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times L}$, lower-triangular, so $y = M x$. Softmax attention picks $M_{tj} = \text{softmax}(q_t^\top k_j / \sqrt{d})$ (row-normalised, dense below the diagonal). Linear attention drops the softmax: $M_{tj} = q_t^\top k_j$.

An SSM does something that looks totally different at first. It runs a recurrence $h_t = A h_{t-1} + B x_t$, $y_t = C h_t$. But unroll it and collect the contribution of $x_j$ to $y_t$:

$$
M_{tj} = C_t \, A^{t-j} \, B_j \quad \text{for } t \geq j.
$$

That's the same $L \times L$ matrix, just built from a recurrence instead of from attention scores. The off-diagonal blocks of this matrix have a specific low-rank structure: for any two disjoint intervals $[a, b]$ and $[c, d]$ with $b < c$, the block $M_{[c:d], [a:b]}$ factors as $(\text{stuff for the right block}) \cdot A^{c-b} \cdot (\text{stuff for the left block})$. Everything flows through the state, which has dimension $N$. So the block has rank at most $N$, regardless of its size.

Matrices with this property are called **semi-separable**. The first plot just shows you the structure: one row of $M$ in log magnitude, and the rank bound sitting flat underneath.

That's the whole structural claim. Semi-separable matrices have a fast parameterisation: $O(LN)$ numbers describe the whole $L \times L$ matrix, and you can multiply by one in $O(LN)$ time via the recurrence or in $O(L^2 / C \cdot N)$ time via a block decomposition where $C$ is the chunk size. The recurrence is the SSM view. The block decomposition is what SSD ships.

## The identity made interactive

If SSMs and linear attention really are the same matrix, you should be able to compute $y = Mx$ either way and get the same answer to machine precision. The second plot does exactly that. Build a toy SSM with state dim $N$, sequence length $L$, random input. Compute $y$ (a) by running the recurrence and (b) by materialising $M$ and doing the matmul. The curve sits on machine epsilon. Noise scales out (both sides of a linear map eat it identically). $N$ and $L$ are free parameters. This is the identity, not a metaphor for the identity. If you slide $N$ up you're adding rank to $M$; if you slide $L$ up you're making it bigger. The two computations still agree.

<Callout tone="insight" title="Softmax attention breaks semi-separability">
  Plain softmax attention is not semi-separable. The row-wise normalisation couples every entry in the row to every other entry, and the off-diagonal blocks don't factor through a finite-dimensional state. *Linear* attention drops softmax, gets the factorisation back, and sits inside the same family as SSMs. Mamba-2's paper makes this explicit: the SSD framework covers "structured masked attention", i.e. linear attention with a causal mask whose structure is semi-separable.
</Callout>

## Where the 2–8× comes from

Once you know $M$ is semi-separable, you get two algorithms for free. The recurrence is $O(LN)$ sequential work. That's great for autoregressive decode (one token at a time, $O(N)$ per step) but terrible for training because it can't fill a GPU (the length dimension is a dep chain). Softmax attention can fill a GPU but costs $O(L^2 d)$ FLOPs and, before [FlashAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14135), $O(L^2)$ HBM traffic.

SSD's block decomposition sits between them. Chop the sequence into chunks of size $C$. Inside a chunk, you do a dense $C \times C$ matmul (because the diagonal block of $M$ is small enough to materialise cheaply). Between chunks, you aggregate state once and pass it along (one $N$-dimensional vector per chunk boundary). The arithmetic becomes matmul-heavy and maps onto tensor cores. The paper reports two comparisons on this plot: 2–8× over Mamba-1's selective scan ("naive SSM scan" in the legend) and parity with FlashAttention at 2k tokens, 6× at 16k ("FlashAttention"). The SSD-vs-FlashAttention multiplier shown at the cursor is the second comparison.

A few things worth trying. Crank $L$ up and watch softmax attention fall off a cliff (that's the $L^2$ term dominating). Pull bandwidth down and FlashAttention suffers more than SSD does (SSD is more compute-bound by construction, because $N$ is big enough to keep the matmul units fed). Slide $N$: at $N = 16$ SSD and FlashAttention are basically on top of each other; at $N = 128$ SSD pulls away because its per-token work scales with $N$ but the memory access pattern doesn't.

<Callout tone="info" title="Two algorithms for one matrix">
  There are really two algorithms, and you pick by sequence length. Short $L$ → use the quadratic-in-$L$ "attention-like" path (dense matmul on the whole triangle). Long $L$ → use the linear-in-$L$ "SSM-like" path (chunked state passing). Both compute the same matrix. Tri Dao's blog post calls this "having your cake and eating it too", which is almost too accurate.
</Callout>

## Selectivity survives

[Mamba](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752) (2023)'s contribution was making $A$ and $B$ depend on the input: $A_t$, $B_t$ instead of constants. That's what "selective" means in "selective SSM". It's also what made the parallel scan kernel hard, because a time-varying recurrence doesn't factor as a power of a matrix anymore. The worry before SSD came out was that the matmul reformulation would force everyone back to time-invariant dynamics.

It doesn't. The semi-separable structure survives when $A$ varies with $t$. The blocks still factor through an $N$-dimensional state; the factors just aren't powers of a single matrix anymore. SSD generalises to this case; the kernel gets a little more complex but the asymptotics are unchanged. Selectivity is what lets the state decide, per token, whether to remember or forget, and you can watch it in a toy recurrence.

Slide `selectivity` from 0 to 1 and the two curves separate. At 0, the selective and non-selective lines coincide (no gating). At 1, the selective state pops up on spikes and collapses on zeros, while the non-selective one exponentially averages everything. The Mamba-2 paper keeps this mechanism and restructures only the kernel; the model behaviour is continuous with Mamba-1.

<Callout tone="aside" title="Hybrid stacks in 2024+">
  Pure SSM stacks lost ground in 2024–2025 to hybrids that interleave a few softmax-attention layers among many SSM/linear-attention layers. Jamba 1.5 and Kimi Linear 2025 are the representative examples. The SSD identity matters here too: you can share a kernel between the "linear" layers even when their parameterisation comes from different families, because semi-separable is the common structure.
</Callout>

Writing this down changed the way I think about the attention-vs-SSM framing. There isn't one. There's a family of causal mixing matrices, softmax attention sits outside of it (the normalisation breaks the factorisation), and everything inside the family (linear attention, S4, Mamba, Mamba-2) is a choice of how to parameterise the same semi-separable structure and which kernel to ship. Cleaner than I thought, and more useful: once you have the matrix, the algorithm engineering is bounded.
